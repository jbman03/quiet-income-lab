# How to Win Your First Commercial Cleaning Contract
> Where small office contracts come from, how to walk through a space, how to price the bid, and what to put in the proposal.

Commercial cleaning (offices, clinics, small shops) gives a residential cleaning business steady, recurring income. Contracts usually run month to month or yearly, visits happen on a fixed schedule, and one good account can be worth more than ten one-time house cleans.

Here's how to land the first one.

## Start small and close to home

Your first contract shouldn't be a hospital or a 50,000 sq ft warehouse. Look for:

- Small offices (insurance agents, accountants, real estate offices, property managers)
- Dental and medical offices, which need reliable, detail-focused cleaners
- Small retail shops, salons and studios
- Offices in buildings you already drive past every week (route density saves fuel and time)

Small businesses often have no cleaning contract at all, or an unhappy one with a large company.

## Find the decision maker

The person who decides is usually the owner, the office manager, or the building's property manager. Walking in during a quiet time, dropping off a one-page flyer, and asking "who handles your cleaning?" still works. So does asking your current residential clients whether their workplace needs a cleaner. Referrals are the warmest leads you'll get.

## Do a walkthrough before you bid

Never bid from square footage alone. Walk the space and note:

- Total cleanable square feet and the floor types (carpet, tile, wood)
- Number of restrooms and break rooms
- How busy it is (staff count, foot traffic, kids, patients)
- Trash and recycling locations, supply storage, after-hours access and alarm codes
- What they're unhappy with now. That's your selling point.

## Price the bid

Commercial bids are usually a monthly price built from labor time:

1. Estimate time per visit from square feet and a production rate (how many square feet per hour your crew cleans), plus restroom time.
2. Multiply by visits per week and 4.33 weeks per month.
3. Multiply hours by your true labor cost (wage plus payroll taxes and insurance), then add supplies.
4. Add your profit margin on top of the total cost.

## Write a simple proposal

One or two pages is enough:

- **Scope:** exactly what's cleaned each visit, and anything weekly or monthly (such as high dusting or baseboards)
- **Schedule:** days and times (after hours is common)
- **Price:** monthly price, what's included (supplies), and payment terms
- **Trust:** proof of insurance, how keys and alarm codes are handled, who to call with a problem
- **Start date** and a simple way to cancel (30 days' notice is common)

## Keep the account

Commercial clients rarely leave because of price. They leave when things get missed. Use the same checklist every visit, reply to complaints the same day, and check in with the office manager once a month. Happy accounts become referrals to the business next door.
